Here, a generative diffusion\r\narchitecture is explored for downscaling such global inputs to km-scale, as a cost-effective machine learning\r\nalternative. The model is trained to predict 2km data from a regional weather model over Taiwan,\r\nconditioned on a 25km global reanalysis. To address the large resolution ratio, different physics involved at\r\ndifferent scales and prediction of channels beyond those in the input data, we employ a two-step approach\r\nwhere a UNet predicts the mean and a corrector diffusion (CorrDiff) model predicts the residual. CorrDiff\r\nexhibits encouraging skill in bulk MAE and CRPS scores. The predicted spectra and distributions from\r\nCorrDiff faithfully recover important power law relationships in the target data. Case studies of coherent\r\nweather phenomena show that CorrDiff can help sharpen wind and temperature gradients that co-locate\r\nwith intense rainfall in cold front, and can help intensify typhoons and synthesize rain band structures.\r\nCalibration of model uncertainty remains challenging. The prospect of unifying methods like CorrDiff\r\nwith coarser resolution global weather models implies a potential for global-to-regional multi-scale machine\r\nlearning simulation.\r\n1 Introduction\r\nCoarse-resolution 25-km global weather prediction is undergoing a machine learning renaissance with the\r\nrecent advance of autoregressive machine learning models trained on global reanalysis [6, 46, 12, 7, 36, 17,\r\n16, 51, 11, 37]. However, many applications of weather and climate data require kilometer-scale forecasts:\r\ne.g., risk assessment and capturing local effects of topography and human land use [25]. Globally, applying\r\nML at km-scale resolution poses significant challenges since training costs are superlinear with respect to\r\nthe resolution of training data. Moreover, predictions from global km-scale physical simulators are not yet\r\nwell tuned, so available training data can have worse systematic biases than coarse-resolution or established\r\nregional simulations [66, 31], and current data tends to cover short periods of time. Such datasets are also\r\nmassive, difficult to transfer between data centers and frequently not produced on machines attached to\r\nsignificant AI computing resources like GPUs.\r\nIn contrast, for regional simulation, using ML to conditionally generate km-scales is attractive. High\u0002quality training data are available as many national weather agencies couple km-scale numerical weather\r\nmodels in a limited domain to coarser resolution global models [20] \u2013 a process called dynamical downscaling.\r\nSince these predictions are augmented by data assimilation from ground-based precipitation radar and other\r\nsensors, good estimate of regional km-scale atmospheric states exists [15]. Such dynamical downscaling is\r\ncomputationally expensive, which limits the number of ensemble members used to quantify uncertainties [45].\r\n1\r\narXiv:2309.15214v4 [cs.LG] 11 Aug 2024 A common inexpensive alternative is to learn a statistical downscaling from these dynamical downscaling\r\nsimulations and observations [72]. This is typically done by learning the values of several parameters of a\r\nstatistical mapping (e.g. quantile mapping, generalized linear regression) that best match a regional high\r\nresolution data [3]. In this context, ML downscaling enters as an advanced (non linear) form of statistical\r\ndownscaling [54] with potential to exceed the fidelity of conventional statistical downscaling.\r\nSeveral ML methods have been previously used for downscaling [9, 60, 22, 68, 45, 71, 2]. Convolutional\r\nNeural Networks have shown promise in globally downscaling climate (100km) data to weather scales (25km)\r\n[42, 56, 3, 53]. However, such deterministic ML approaches require interventions to produce useful probabilistic\r\nresults, such as ensemble inference [56] or predicting the parameters of an assumed distribution [3]).\r\nThe stochastic nature of atmospheric physics at km-scale [61] renders downscaling inherently probabilistic,\r\nmaking it natural to explore generative models at these scales. Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)\r\nhave been tested, including for forecasting precipitation at km-scale in various regions [39, 50, 26, 55, 24, 71];\r\nsee the latter for a good review. Training GANs, however, poses several practical challenges including mode\r\ncollapse, training instabilities, and difficulties in capturing long tails of distributions [73, 35, 58].\r\nAlternatively, diffusion models offer training stability [29, 18] alongside demonstrable skill in probabilisti\u0002cally generating km-scales. [1] used a diffusion model for predicting rain density in the UK from vorticity as\r\nan input, thus demonstrating potential for channel synthesis. [27] used a diffusion model for downscaling\r\nsolar irradiance in Hawaii with a 1 day lead time, demonstrating the ability to simultaneously forecast.\r\nMoreover, diffusion models have been used directly for probabilistic weather forecasting and nowcasting\r\n[38, 40, 43, 67] \u2013 including global ensemble predictions that outperform conventional weather prediction on a\r\nrange of important stochastic metrics at 0.25-degree resolution [51]. As a proof of concept we will demonstrate such a\r\nML model trained for the region surrounding Taiwan.\r\nDetails follow. The key contributions of this paper are:\r\n1. A physics-inspired, two-step approach (CorrDiff) to simultaneously learn mappings between low- and\r\nhigh-resolution weather data across multiple variables with high fidelity alongside new channel synthesis.\r\n2. For the case studies considered, CorrDiff adds physically realistic improvements to the representation of\r\nunder-resolved coherent weather phenomena \u2013 frontal systems and typhoons.\r\n3. CorrDiff is sample-efficient, learning effectively from just 3 years of data.\r\n4. This approach showed slow convergence and resulted in poor-quality images\r\nwith incoherent structures. This\r\nissue is particularly relevant for the downscaling task, which must account for large spatial shifts, correct\r\nbiases in static features like topography, and synthesize entirely new channels like radar reflectivity. CorrDiff consists of two steps: regression and generation. The regression step approximates\r\nthe mean, while the generation step further corrects the mean but also generates the distribution, producing\r\nfine-scale details stochastically. This approach is akin to the decomposition of physical variables into their\r\nmean and perturbations, common practice in fluid dynamics, e.g. [47].\r\nThrough extensive testing in the region of Taiwan, the model is shown to produce reasonably realistic\r\npower spectra and probability distributions of all target variables. The diffusion component of CorrDiff is\r\nfound to be especially important for the task of radar channel synthesis. Several case studies reveal that the\r\nmodel is able to downscale coherent structures consistently across its variables. Focusing on a midlatitude\r\nfrontal event, horizontally co-located gradients of winds and temperatures are generated alongside spatially\r\nconsistent radar reflectivity features, with incomplete but improved sharpness. For typhoons, encouraging\r\npartial corrections of typhoon size and wind speed intensity are found, alongside generated radar echos\r\ncontaining qualitatively realistic km-scale details reminiscent of tropical cyclone rainband morphology. It is\r\nlogical to expect that the model\u2019s accuracy could be further improved with a larger training dataset that\r\ncontains more diverse examples of such rare coherent structures such as by pre-training on large libraries of\r\ntyphoons generated by high-resolution physical simulators; we encourage work in this direction.\r\nAnother important unsolved challenge is optimally calibrating CorrDiff\u2019s generated uncertainty to better\r\nmatch its error levels. This is somewhat unexpected since diffusion models for image generation are known\r\nto be over-dispersive in the sense of producing low-quality samples and variance-reducing techniques are\r\noften used during the sampling to discourage such outliers [30, 34]. The lack of grid-point-level spread here\r\ncould owe to a number of factors in the diffusion training process including the noise schedules used, the\r\ncomparatively large resolution (448x488) compared to typical image generation (64x64), or the weighting in\r\nthe loss function.\r\nTo become useful for km-scale weather prediction, extensions of CorrDiff are encouraged that include\r\ntemporal coherence, such as via video diffusion or learnt autoregressive km-scale dynamics; as with super\u0002resolution these must be formulated as stochastic machine learning tasks. Currently, beyond the coherence\r\nof the large scale conditioning given from ERA5 there is no guarantee that CorrDiff\u2019s km-scale dynamics\r\nwill be coherent in time. Additional integrations with km-scale data assimilation are also essential for this\r\nuse case. Our current demonstration relies only on the global data assimilation (DA) used to produce the\r\nERA5 dataset. Unlike the target data it is trained on, CorrDiff effectively bypasses the regional DA, which\r\nfor CWA is of similar computational cost as running the operational numerical model (WRF) model for 13h.\r\nFor such extensions, the two step approach in CorrDiff offers practical advantages to reduce the amount of\r\nvariance that must be handled stochastically, and trade-off between the fast inference of the mean using the\r\nUNet, and the probabilistic inference of the CorrDiff. This is particularly useful given that some variables\r\ndepend more than others on the diffusion step for their skill (see Figure 2). Moreover, it could be possible to\r\napply the diffusion step to a mean prediction obtained in a different way (e.g. a numerical model if available)\r\nto generate a plausible distribution from a single prediction.\r\nWith the current hardware and code-base CorrDiff inference is about 652 times faster, and 1, 310 times\r\nmore energy efficient than running CWA-WRF on CPUs, although such a comparison between dynamical\r\nand statistical downscaling is limited (see 6.3 for details). This paper focused on generation quality, and not\r\non optimal inference speed, for which gains could be easily anticipated. Our CorrDiff prototype is using a\r\n11 dozen iterations thanks to the initial regression step. Refinement of the technique could reduce the number of\r\niterations to only a few by using distillation methods [59, 73, 74] and pursuing other performance optimization\r\ntechniques [41, 69].\r\nIf some of the above challenges in the model are resolved, several potential extensions of the proposed\r\nmethod are worth consideration by the community:\r\n1. Downscaling Coarse-Resolution Medium-Range Forecasts: This requires addressing lead\r\ntime-dependent forecast errors in the input, enabling a comprehensive evaluation of simultaneous\r\nbias correction and downscaling, and adding temporal coherence and km-scale prediction and data\r\nassimilation capabilities.\r\n2. Downscaling at Different Geographic Locations: The primary obstacle here is the scarcity of\r\nreliable kilometer-scale weather data. Additionally, addressing the computational scalability of CorrDiff\r\nfor regions significantly larger than Taiwan is crucial.\r\n3. Downscaling Future Climate Predictions: This introduces further complexities related to con\u0002ditioning probabilistic predictions on various future anthropogenic emissions scenarios and assessing\r\nwhether the generated weather envelope appropriately reflects climate sensitivity, particularly concerning\r\nextreme events.\r\n4. Synthesizing sub-km sensor observations: To achieve effective resolutions beyond what is possible\r\nto simulate today, and sidestep issues of numerical simulation, it would be interesting to test whether\r\nvariants of CorrDiff can be trained to generate raw senor observations where dense networks exists.\r\nOur demonstrated ability to synthesize an observable as challenging as radar reflectivity from column\r\nwater vapor should embolden such efforts.\r\nThese extensions have significant potential benefits such as accelerated regional forecasts, increased\r\nensemble sizes, improved climate downscaling, and the provision of high-resolution regional forecasts in\r\ndata-scarce regions, leveraging training data from adjacent areas.\r\n5 Methods\r\nThis section elaborates on the proposed CorrDiff methodology for probabilistic downscaling. The choice of region is driven\r\nby our partnership with a local government agency (CWA) which operates a regional dynamical downscaling.\r\nHowever, the region of Taiwan also presents a unique diversity of meteorological conditions and phenomena\r\nsuch as tropical cyclones (i.e., typhoons), mid-latitude cyclones which generate weather fronts, and steep\r\ntopography with snow-capped mountains and land-sea contrast. Such diversity is hard to find elsewhere in\r\nsuch a relatively small domain.\r\nThe input (conditioning) dataset is taken from ERA5 reanalysis [28], a global dataset at spatial resolution\r\nof about 25km and a temporal resolution of 1h, the latter matches the target data listed below. To facilitate\r\ntraining, we interpolate the input data onto the curvilinear grid of CWA with bilinear interpolation (with a\r\nrate of 4x), which results in 36\u00d736 pixels over the region of Taiwan. Each sample in the input dataset consists\r\nof 12 channels of information; see Table S2. This includes two pressure levels (500 hPa and 850 hPa) with\r\nfour corresponding variables: temperature, eastward and northward components of the horizontal wind vector,\r\nand geopotential height. Additionally, the dataset includes single-level variables such as 2-meter Temperature,\r\n10-meter wind vector and total column water vapor. This input channel set is admittedly somewhat arbitrary,\r\nbut serves the purposes of (i) allowing a reasonable sparse representation of the thermodynamic state of the\r\nmacro-scale atmosphere, while (ii) intentionally including only limited information about atmospheric water\r\nvia the total vertical integral of an invisible trace gas - water vapor. This ensures the task of synthesizing\r\nradar reflectivity - a much more complex observable that relates to the sixth moment of the cloud water\r\ndroplet distribution - is appropriately ambitious, as a strong test of CorrDiff\u2019s generative component.\r\nThe target dataset used in this study is a subset of the proprietary RWRF model data (Radar Data\r\nAssimilation with WRFDA 1). The RWRF model is one of the operational regional Numerical Weather\r\nPrediction (NWP) models developed by CWA [15], which focuses on radar Data Assimilation (DA) in the\r\nvicinity of Taiwan. Assimilating radar data is a common strategy used in regional weather prediction, which\r\nhelps constrain especially stochastic convective processes such as mesoscale convective systems and short-lived\r\nthunderstorms. In addition, CWA assimilates several surface measurements to complement the radar data\r\nthat often miss the surface values. The WRF-CWA system uses a nested 2km domain in a larger 10km\r\ndomain that is driven by a global model (GFS) as boundary conditions [15]. By incorporating radar data\r\n[14], RWRF improves the short-term prediction of high-impact weather events. The radar observations\r\npossess high spatial resolution of approximately 1km and temporal resolutions of 5-10 minutes at a convective\r\nscale. These observations provide useful wind information (radial velocity) as well as hydrometeors (radar\r\nreflectivity), with a particular emphasis on the lower atmosphere. The radar data assimilation relies on the\r\navailability of reliable and precise observations, which contributes significantly to enhance the accuracy and\r\nperformance of the applied deep learning algorithms in the context of NWP applications.\r\nThe target dataset covers the years 2018-2021. It has a temporal frequency of one hour and a spatial\r\nresolution of 2km. We use only the inner (nested) 2km domain, which has 448 \u00d7 448 pixels, projected using\r\nthe Lambert conformal conical projection around Taiwan. The geographical extent of the dataset spans from\r\napproximately 116.371\u00b0E to 125.568\u00b0E in longitude and 19.5483\u00b0N to 27.8446\u00b0N in latitude. We sub-selected\r\n4 channels (variables) as the target variables that are most relevant for practical forecasting: temperature at\r\n2 meter above the surface, the horizontal winds at 10 meter above the surface and the 1h maximum derived\r\nradar reflectivity (radar reflectivity hereafter) - a surrogate of the expected precipitation. Notably, the radar\r\nreflectivity channel is not present in the input data, and needs to be predicted based on the other channels\r\n(channel synthesis). The radar reflectivity data also exhibits a distinct distribution compared to the other\r\n1https://www.mmm.ucar.edu/models/wrfda\r\n14 output channels, with positively skewed values and a prominent zero-mode consistent with typical non-raining\r\nconditions.\r\nInitially, the target data is provided in the NetCDF format, which is the output of the WRFDA assimilation\r\nprocess. A vertical interpolation from hybrid coordinates (i.e., sigma levels) to pressure coordinates (i.e.,\r\nisobaric levels) is applied. As part of the preprocessing steps, the data is converted to the Hadoop Distributed\r\nFile System (HDFS) format. Additionally, any missing or corrupted data points represented by \"inf\" or \"nan\"\r\nvalues are eliminated from the dataset. This leads to a reduction in the number of images from 37,944 to\r\n33,813. See 2 for more details.\r\nTo avoid over-fitting, we divide the data into training and testing sets. Three years of data 2018-2020 are\r\nused for training (24,154 images total) and 2021 is used for testing. Some selected dates from 2022 and 2023\r\nare used for case studies.\r\n5.3.2 Network architecture and training\r\nThe CorrDiff method has two step learning approach. To ensure compatibility and consistency, we employ\r\nthe same UNet architecture for both the regression and diffusion networks. We enhance the UNet of [63] by\r\nincreasing its size to include 6 encoder layers and 6 decoder layers. The base embedding size is set to 128, and\r\nit is multiplied over channels according to the multipliers [1,2,2,2,2]. The attention resolution is defined as 28.\r\nTo represent time (i.e., timesteps in the diffusion process, not to be confused with the time of day), we utilize\r\nthe Fourier-based position embedding. This addition provides\r\nmore context and global information to the diffusion process, potentially improving its convergence..
